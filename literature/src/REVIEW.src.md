# Human subtelomeres: a structured literature review

Human chromosome ends are mosaics of duplicated blocks shared among non-homologous arms, and three decades of locus-level work established that these blocks move between ends, that whole terminal segments can be replaced, and that telomere length is regulated in part by the end on which a telomere sits. What the literature has not settled is how often terminal replacement alleles arise, whether they form by reciprocal or one-way transfer, how old they are, and whether exchange is ongoing at rates visible in pedigrees. Population-scale assembly catalogues addressing these questions appeared only in 2026, as preprints ([[10.64898/2026.07.10.737660]]; [[10.64898/2026.09.03.748401]]). This review covers {{n_records}} included records, selected from {{n_candidates}} retrieved candidates ({{n_rel3}} graded as directly relevant); the search strategy is in [search_log.json](search_log.json) and the per-record annotations are in [records.tsv](records.tsv).

## Architecture and exchange

### Duplicon patchworks at chromosome ends

The defining feature of a human subtelomere is a patchwork of segmental duplications whose copies sit near the ends of several unrelated chromosomes ([[10.1038/nrg727]]). Early cloning of telomere-associated DNA already showed repeat families shared among ends and allelic polymorphism next to the TTAGGG tract ([[10.1016/0092-8674(90)90293-n]]; [[10.1128/mcb.10.2.518]]). Sequence-level work then resolved a recurring two-part organisation: a distal domain of short, interrupted homologies to many ends and a proximal domain of 10 to 40 kb blocks shared with few ends, often separated by degenerate internal (TTAGGG)n tracts ([[10.1093/hmg/6.8.1305]]; [[10.1038/ng0397-252]]). Reference-era assemblies of the 41 distinct telomere regions quantified the patchwork ([[10.1101/gr.1245004]]), and duplicon analysis showed that subtelomeric repeats make up most of the distal 100 kb and that a small set of subterminal duplicon families accounts for all telomere-adjacent sequence then known ([[10.1186/gb-2007-8-7-r151]]). Gap-filled assemblies with a multiread annotation pipeline were the last reference-based update before complete genomes ([[10.1101/gr.166983.113]]).

These blocks are polymorphic in presence, copy number and location. An olfactory-receptor-containing block is fixed at three ends but present polymorphically at eleven others, with frequencies that differ among populations ([[10.1093/hmg/7.1.13]]), and SNP analysis of its copies shows recent exchange between non-homologous ends ([[10.1093/hmg/10.21.2363]]). The 16p tip carries discrete length alleles differing by up to 260 kb that segregate as stable Mendelian variants ([[10.1016/0092-8674(91)90243-r]]). FISH surveys with subtelomeric probes found widespread segmental polymorphism consistent with both reciprocal translocation and homology-driven spreading ([[10.1101/gr.322802]]; [[10.1016/s0888-7543(05)80125-9]]). The clearest early case of a transposed terminal allele is a telomere plus adjacent DNA present on a minority of 16p and 16q chromosomes, which must have relocated together with its telomere-adjacent sequence ([[10.1093/hmg/8.9.1637]]).

Comparative data place much of this turnover in recent primate history. About half of the known subtelomeric sequence was inferred to be human-specific, formed through repeated translocations between ends ([[10.1038/nature04029]]). The 15q end, created by a fission in the great-ape ancestor, has since exchanged material with other subtelomeres, including a transfer of at least 200 kb to 4q in African apes ([[10.1101/gr.083170.108]]). Complete ape genomes now show lineage-specific subterminal satellite caps with independent origins in Pan and gorilla ([[10.1101/gr.136556.111]]; [[10.1101/gr.280987.125]]; [[10.1038/s41586-025-08816-3]]) and distinct subtelomeric satellite architectures in macaque ([[10.1016/j.cell.2026.02.018]]). Yeast long-read assemblies show the same pattern of subtelomeres as the fastest-rearranging compartment of the genome ([[10.1038/ng.3847]]).

### Mechanisms of terminal transfer

Two mechanistic models compete. The first is reciprocal or non-homologous exchange: breakpoint analysis of human subtelomeric duplications concluded that non-homologous end-joining predominates in creating them, with homology-based transfers acting on blocks once duplicated ([[10.1038/nature04029]]). The second is one-way copying of a donor end onto a broken recipient. In yeast, a one-ended break invades a distant chromosome end with as little as 72 bp of homology and copies it to the telomere by break-induced replication (BIR), producing a nonreciprocal translocation and telomere capture ([[10.1093/genetics/150.3.1037]]). BIR is now well characterised as a conservative, mutagenic, template-switching process ([[10.4161/cc.7.7.5613]]; [[10.1016/j.tig.2018.04.002]]; [[10.1038/nature06047]]), whose microhomology-mediated form explains complex human CNVs with 2 to 5 bp junction microhomology ([[10.1371/journal.pgen.1000327]]). Whether a subtelomeric break is repaired by gene conversion or BIR depends on its distance from the end and on resection and chromatin ([[10.15252/embj.201796631]]).

Clinical cytogenetics supplies direct human examples of one-way terminal replacement. Apparent terminal deletions are often cryptic subtelomeric translocations stabilised by capture of another chromosome's telomere ([[10.1038/ng0793-252]]), and capture is frequent among inverted duplication-deletions and 1p36 derivatives ([[10.1159/000315887]]; [[10.1159/000068536]]; [[10.1007/s00439-003-1029-y]]). The 1p36 breakpoints lack homology or low-copy repeats, which pointed to BIR rather than non-allelic homologous recombination ([[10.1007/s00439-003-1029-y]]). The alternative outcome, direct telomere healing by de novo TTAGGG addition, was first shown on a truncated 16p ([[10.1038/346868a0]]), and its frequency relative to interstitial telomere retention has been surveyed in constitutional rearrangements ([[10.1159/000230002]]). A 16p break stabilised by recombination between two related Alu elements, one of them subtelomeric, is the closest human precedent for a homology-mediated terminal replacement in the germline ([[10.1093/hmg/5.8.1163]]). Junction sequencing of pathogenic subtelomeric rearrangements found a mix of end-joining, repeat-mediated recombination and replication-based signatures ([[10.1093/hmg/ddr293]]), and long-read assemblies have begun to assign repair signatures to large population subtelomeric SVs ([[10.1093/nar/gkae1294]]). Mitotic sister chromatid exchange is also strongly elevated at chromosome ends ([[10.1371/journal.pgen.0030032]]).

### Interstitial telomeric sequences at junctions

Short internal TTAGGG-like tracts are enriched more than 25-fold in subtelomeres ([[10.1101/gr.1245004]]) and sit at duplicon boundaries, which led to the proposal that they mark old junctions ([[10.1186/gb-2007-8-7-r151]]). Human ITSs fall into short, subtelomeric and fusion classes ([[10.1007/s004120100135]]). Comparative primate analysis shows short ITSs were inserted suddenly at double-strand-break repair sites rather than grown by expansion ([[10.1101/gr.2778904]]), possibly via telomerase ([[10.1186/gb-2007-8-12-r260]]), whereas the 2q13 fusion ITS records an ancestral telomere-telomere fusion ([[10.1073/pnas.88.20.9051]]; [[10.1101/gr.337602]]). ITSs are length-polymorphic and unstable ([[10.1006/geno.2000.6280]]; [[10.3390/genes10020118]]; [[10.1159/000167807]]). In yeast, BIR that runs into an ITS frequently terminates there and seeds an ectopic telomere ([[10.1093/nar/gkaa1081]]), and ITSs raise gross rearrangement rates through several pathways ([[10.1073/pnas.2407314121]]). This gives a testable prediction: if terminal replacements arise by BIR-like copying, junctions should carry donor homology and sometimes ITS remnants.

### Model systems: 4q/10q, PAR1, acrocentrics

The 4q/10q system is the best-dated subtelomere exchange. Exchange of D4Z4 units between 4q35 and 10q26 was inferred from Southern data ([[10.1093/hmg/5.12.1997]]), translocated arrays were found in a fifth of Dutch donors ([[10.1093/hmg/9.19.2879]]), and translocation frequencies vary by population ([[10.1186/1471-2377-2-7]]). Haplotype analysis in HapMap and HGDP panels then showed that all 4q and 10q subtelomeres trace to only four discrete transfer events, all present before the out-of-Africa dispersal ([[10.1016/j.ajhg.2010.01.035]]). This is the main precedent for treating subtelomere exchange alleles as few and old rather than recurrent. The 4qA and 4qB distal variants, and the permissive haplotype required for FSHD, show that such alleles have phenotypic consequences ([[10.1038/ng999]]; [[10.1126/science.1189044]]), and engineered 4q/10q exchange reverts the disease genotype ([[10.1126/sciadv.adl1922]]). T2T-era work warns that D4Z4-like repeats extend beyond these two loci ([[10.1038/s41431-025-02000-x]]).

PAR1 is the one human terminal region with obligatory meiotic exchange, with a male crossover rate far above the genome average ([[10.2174/138920207780368141]]; [[10.1371/journal.pgen.1004503]]). Its boundary is itself polymorphic: an X-to-Y insertional translocation creates an extended PAR1 in a subset of men ([[10.1371/journal.pgen.1004578]]; [[10.1371/journal.pgen.1007680]]), and the boundary position remains debated ([[10.1016/j.ajhg.2024.09.005]]; [[10.1534/genetics.114.172692]]). The Xp/Yp telomere junction carries a few highly diverged haplotypes in strong linkage disequilibrium ([[10.1002/j.1460-2075.1995.tb00227.x]]; [[10.1093/hmg/6.13.2291]]).

The acrocentric short arms are the precedent for pangenome inference of heterologous exchange: shared pseudo-homologous regions in assemblies were interpreted as evidence of ongoing recombination between non-homologous p-arms ([[10.1038/s41586-023-05976-y]]). A four-generation pedigree later found allelic p-arm recombination depleted and a single ectopic event in 107 transmissions ([[10.1016/j.cell.2026.05.035]]), a calibration that population inference alone cannot supply.

## Telomere length at individual ends

### Arm-specific and allele-specific length

That telomere length depends on the chromosome end is established. Q-FISH revealed large length heterogeneity among telomeres within a cell ([[10.1093/hmg/5.5.685]]) and consistently short 17p telomeres ([[10.1038/ng0198-018]]); homologues differ in length ([[10.1093/nar/29.15.3164]]), relative lengths of individual telomeres are fixed in the zygote ([[10.1111/j.1474-9728.2004.00093.x]]), and allelic relative lengths segregate in families ([[10.1007/s00439-006-0137-x]]). STELA demonstrated extensive allelic length variation ([[10.1038/ng1084]]) and, across 2p, 11q, 12q, 17p and XpYp, end-specific erosion and stability that hTERT overexpression erases, the basis for the claim of cis-acting determinants ([[10.1093/hmg/ddi486]]). Long-read methods confirmed end-specific lengths conserved across individuals ([[10.1126/science.ado0431]]), with arm explaining about 9% of length variance in more than 2,500 All of Us participants ([[10.1038/s41467-026-71172-x]]), across tissues and populations ([[10.64898/2025.12.10.693405]]) and across 232 HPRC individuals ([[10.64898/2026.08.19.745664]]), and trio studies show inheritance of arm-level length with parent-of-origin effects ([[10.1101/2025.10.07.680721]]; [[10.64898/2025.12.14.693655]]).

The candidate cis determinant has shifted from the subtelomere to the telomere-proximal variant repeats. Heritable telomere variant sequences explain much of allele-specific length, and deleting them resets length ([[10.1038/s41467-026-77310-9]]); a 212-individual atlas finds a structured, heritable variant-repeat code at nearly every end, modulated by subtelomeric TAR1 elements ([[10.64898/2026.05.01.722200]]). In yeast, subtelomeric heterochromatin sets a telomere-specific length that moves with the distal 15 kb to another end ([[10.1093/nar/gkaf1457]]). Evidence that the subtelomere allele itself sets length in humans is weak. Optical mapping proposed haplotype-linked length measurement but did not test it ([[10.1093/nar/gkx017]]). The 860-assembly catalogue found only suggestive associations between terminal haplotype and length at 1p, 5q and 6q (FDR < 0.1, all other arms non-significant) ([[10.64898/2026.09.03.748401]]). The atlas paired test of foreign versus own terminal alleles within individuals finds no positive cis effect: boundary-robust estimates are {{tl_ext_min}} to +{{tl_ext_max}} bp (all p ≥ {{tl_ext_min_p}}). A positive association obtained with a telomere-length-dependent classification window was an artefact of that window ([NOVELTY.md](NOVELTY.md)).

### Long-read measurement methods

Chromosome-end-resolved length is now routine from long reads. Telogator anchors reads to subtelomere references ([[10.1093/bioinformatics/btac005]]), and Telogator2 uses variant repeats to separate all 92 alleles ([[10.1186/s12859-024-05807-5]]). Capture-based PacBio HiFi ([[10.1038/s41467-023-35823-7]]), nanopore Telomere Profiling ([[10.1126/science.ado0431]]) and Telo-seq ([[10.1038/s41467-024-48917-7]]) give single-telomere resolution, with additional pipelines and benchmarks appearing ([[10.1186/s13059-025-03783-4]]; [[10.1371/journal.pcbi.1013915]]; [[10.1101/2025.01.29.635538]]). A known pitfall is systematic nanopore miscalling of telomeric repeats ([[10.1186/s13059-022-02751-6]]), and every read-assignment method depends on the subtelomere reference used, so mis-specified terminal alleles propagate into length calls.

## Resources and theory

### Complete genomes and pangenomes

T2T-CHM13 provided the first complete set of human ends ([[10.1126/science.abj6987]]) together with a complete segmental duplication map ([[10.1126/science.abj6965]]). The HG002 Q100 diploid benchmark adds a second, independently validated pair of haplotypes ([[10.1016/j.cell.2026.06.016]]). HPRC Release 1 delivered 47 phased diploid assemblies ([[10.1038/s41586-023-05896-x]]) and Release 2 expanded this about fivefold ([[10.64898/2026.07.21.739710]]), built with hifiasm and Verkko ([[10.1038/s41592-020-01056-5]]; [[10.1038/s41587-023-01662-6]]). HGSVC3 and population SD surveys extend complete-region SV analysis ([[10.1038/s41586-025-09140-6]]; [[10.1038/s41588-024-02051-8]]). Assembly breaks and misjoins concentrate in SD-rich regions ([[10.1101/gr.277334.122]]), so subtelomeric calls from assemblies require orthogonal read-level and pedigree validation. Multi-generation T2T pedigrees now allow direct observation of transmission ([[10.1038/s41586-025-08922-2]]; [[10.64898/2025.12.14.693655]]).

### Existing subtelomere catalogues

Before complete assemblies, subtelomere resources were clone sets for clinical FISH ([[10.1038/ng0996-86]]; [[10.1086/302998]]) and reference assemblies ([[10.1101/gr.1245004]]; [[10.1101/gr.166983.113]]). The first population catalogue of long-range subtelomere haplotypes used optical maps of 154 genomes from 26 populations and estimated a timeline for duplicon spread ([[10.1371/journal.pgen.1008347]]; [[10.1093/nar/gkx017]]), and targeted assembly methods followed ([[10.1186/s12859-022-05081-3]]). In 2026, three assembly-scale preprints appeared. One compares all ends across 465 assemblies and groups them into sequence communities ([[10.64898/2026.07.10.737660]]). A second releases complete subtelomere sequences for all non-acrocentric arms from 860 haploid assemblies, reporting multiple haplotypes differing by 100 to 200 kb on most autosomal arms, arm-not-ancestry structure, conservation of blocks in great apes, and rare recombination ([[10.64898/2026.09.03.748401]]), with sequences deposited publicly ([[10.5281/zenodo.21807155]]). A third catalogues telomere arrays rather than subtelomeres ([[10.64898/2026.05.01.722200]]). An assembly-based population catalogue of human subtelomere structure therefore already exists in preprint form, including multi-haplotype arms, conservation of shared blocks in great apes and gene-content differences between haplotypes. A new atlas must define its contribution against it. One caution applies to every such catalogue: the fraction of ends called foreign depends on how much terminal sequence is classified. In the atlas it is {{foreign_pct_S5}}%, {{foreign_pct_S10}}% and {{foreign_pct_S20}}% for the first 5, 10 and 20 kb after the telomere boundary.

### Concerted evolution as a frame

The 2026 subtelomere preprint interprets shared terminal sequence as concerted evolution driven by recurrent ectopic exchange ([[10.64898/2026.07.10.737660]]). The concept comes from repeated-DNA theory: unequal exchange and gene conversion homogenise copies so that a family evolves as a unit ([[10.1126/science.1251186]]; [[10.1038/299111a0]]; [[10.1016/0040-5809(83)90015-1]]; [[10.1086/419073]]; [[10.1086/302221]]). Interlocus gene conversion is measurable in human SDs ([[10.1371/journal.pone.0075949]]; [[10.1038/s41586-023-05895-y]]). The competing model, birth-and-death evolution, predicts discrete copies created by duplication that persist or are lost independently ([[10.1146/annurev.genet.39.073003.112240]]). The two make different predictions for subtelomeres: concerted exchange predicts recurrent, independently formed junctions and frequent de novo events, whereas birth-and-death predicts a small number of old alleles with shared junctions that are transmitted intact. The 4q/10q data fit the second pattern ([[10.1016/j.ajhg.2010.01.035]]), and the RNU2 array shows that a repeat can evolve concertedly almost entirely within a chromosome lineage, with interchromosomal exchange rare ([[10.1093/emboj/16.3.588]]).

### Where the atlas stands

The atlas resolves several of the open points above; details and statistics are in [NOVELTY.md](NOVELTY.md).

On mechanism, the donor ends of carriers are no more often reciprocal than those of non-carriers ({{recip_pct_carriers}}% vs {{recip_pct_noncarriers}}%, p = {{recip_chi2_p}}). Of {{jx_clusters}} switch clusters, {{jx_spanning_pct}}% lie inside donor-recipient homology. This favours one-way, homology-directed copying over the end-joining-dominated model ([[10.1038/nature04029]]). Interstitial telomeric sequences are not enriched at switches more than 5 kb from the telomere.

On age, carriers form tight clusters for most testable forms ({{age_forms_clustered_crit}} of {{age_forms_analysed}}; {{age_forms_untestable}} of {{age_forms_selected}} selected forms could not be tested), but single origin is supported for only {{age_single_origin}} of {{age_forms_analysed}}, and none is strictly monophyletic. Median clock ages are 0.2 to 1.5 Myr for {{age_clustered_02_15}} of {{age_clustered}} clustered forms, and none of {{ape_clusters}} recurrent switch configurations is present in the T2T great-ape genomes. This is consistent with the 4q/10q picture of few, old transfers ([[10.1016/j.ajhg.2010.01.035]]) for a subset of forms; it does not establish single origin genome-wide.

On telomere length, the terminal allele does not detectably change the adjacent telomere's length, and pedigree transmission is overwhelmingly intact ({{ped_foreign_intact}} of {{ped_foreign}} foreign ends).

On reproducibility, the revision-2 catalogue ({{cat_alleles}} structural alleles, {{cat_haplogroups}} haplogroups) agrees with the Tan et al. sequences of the same haplotypes for {{tan_sa_agree}}% of alleles. All {{tan_rep_n}} common tier-A alleles recur in four independent cohorts ([[10.1038/s41586-025-09140-6]]; [[10.1038/s41586-023-06173-7]]), and non-native allele frequencies there match Release 2 (r = {{tan_freq_r}}). The mechanism, age and telomere-length analyses used the earlier per-window form calls, whereas the catalogue labels foreign blocks by paralog family, so form counts differ between the two.

Two caveats bound these results. Atlas switch points are changes in paralog similarity within shared sequence, so they do not pin down structural breakpoints. Gene conversion can blur both single-origin calls and ages.
